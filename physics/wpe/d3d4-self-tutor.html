Skill 1 of 6 · Multi-force work and energy

🛴 Every force counts

A push helps, friction slows, gravity pulls—and your energy calculation must include them all.

Core1A · Guided example

A box being pulled across a floor

A 2 kg box already moves right at 1 m/s. A child pulls right with 10 N. Friction pulls left with 4 N. The box moves 3 m right on a level floor.

  1. Choose the object: the box. Draw the four forces and the direction of its movement.
  2. Do the signs first: pull helps movement (+), friction opposes it (−), gravity and normal do zero work during horizontal motion.
  3. Make a work ledger:
    ForceWork
    Pull+10 × 3 = +30 J
    Friction−4 × 3 = −12 J
    Gravity0 J
    Normal support0 J
  4. Add all the work: net work = +30 − 12 = +18 J. This—not friction alone—equals the change in kinetic energy.
  5. Find speed: initial K = ½ × 2 × 1² = 1 J, final K = 19 J. So final speed = √19 ≈ 4.36 m/s.

Remember: Every force gets a ledger row. Add the signed work before using the change in kinetic energy.

When can I use this method?

The forces are constant over 3 m, air drag is ignored, and the box stays level. If it climbs upward, gravity also does negative work. The normal force is perpendicular to this horizontal movement, so its work is zero.

Skill 2 of 6 · Spring contact and energy

🌀 When the spring stops pushing

One journey can have two different sets of forces. Find the point where the rules change.

Core1A · Guided example

A spring pushes a toy cart

A 1 kg cart begins at rest against a spring compressed by 0.20 m with stiffness 100 N/m. The cart is not attached. The whole track is smooth.

  1. Divide the path: A = spring contact. B = after the spring returns to natural length. The spring cannot pull this unattached cart.
  2. Energy at the start: spring energy = ½kx² = ½ × 100 × 0.20² = 2 J.
  3. At release: the 2 J becomes kinetic energy, so ½ × 1 × v² = 2. The cart leaves at 2 m/s.
  4. Later on smooth ground: the spring no longer acts and friction is zero, so the cart continues at 2 m/s.

Remember: Write one energy rule while contact exists, then check which forces act afterward. Do not continue adding spring work after separation.

When can I use this method?

The spring is ideal and not attached, the track is smooth, and energy loss is ignored. If rough ground begins later, friction belongs in that later region only.

Core1B · Different challenge

The cart crosses a rough strip

A 2 kg toy cart begins at rest against an unattached spring of stiffness 200 N/m, compressed by 0.10 m. The launch is smooth until the spring reaches natural length. Then the cart immediately crosses a 0.50 m rough strip with 1 N friction. No motor acts. Does it cross, and what is its speed at the far end?

Skill 3 of 6 · Constant power

⚡ Energy added each second

A motor can supply equal amounts of energy every second without making speed rise equally every second.

Core1A · Guided example

A powered toy sled

A 2 kg sled already moves right at 2 m/s. A motor supplies a steady 12 watts of mechanical power on a smooth, level track, for 4 seconds. Assume there is no friction.

  1. Read watts: 12 W means the motor adds 12 J of energy per second, not 12 N of force.
  2. Start with energy already there: K at the start = ½ × 2 × 2² = 4 J.
  3. Keep an energy table: K after t seconds = 4 + 12 × t joules. At 1 s, K = 16 J. At 4 s, K = 52 J.
  4. Turn energy into speed: K = ½mv², so speed after 1 s is 4 m/s and after 4 s is √52 ≈ 7.21 m/s.
  5. Choose graph shapes: kinetic energy against time is a rising straight line. Speed against time rises but gradually flattens. Distance against time grows with an increasing slope because the sled speeds up.

Remember: constant power means equal energy each second—not a constant force or constant acceleration. Always include the energy from the initial speed.

When does this work?

The supplied 12 W is useful mechanical power, the mass stays fixed, the sled is already moving, and there are no energy losses. We only need an energy table and graph shapes; no calculus or memorized distance–time power law is needed.

Core1B · Different challenge

Skill 4 of 6 · Changing centre of mass

⛓️ Only the hanging part

When a rope hangs over an edge, the amount of hanging rope—and the position of its middle—both change as you pull.

Core1A · Guided example

Lift a chain onto a table

A uniform 4 m chain has mass 2 kg. Initially 2 m hangs below a smooth table edge; the rest lies on the table. Pull it very slowly until none hangs. Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. Find mass per metre: 2 kg ÷ 4 m = 0.5 kg/m.
  2. Use the hanging part: 2 m hangs, so its mass is 0.5 × 2 = 1 kg.
  3. Find its middle: because it is uniform, its centre of mass is halfway down—1 m below the table.
  4. Compare energies: choose table height as zero. Initially the hanging part has U = −1 × 10 × 1 = −10 J. At the end, U = 0 J.
  5. Find the work: the helper works slowly, so the work needed is the increase in potential energy: 0 − (−10) = +10 J.

Remember: as the hanging length shrinks, both its mass and its centre-of-mass depth shrink. Compare two pictures rather than lifting the entire chain as one fixed lump.

When can I use this method?

The chain is uniform and flexible, the edge and tabletop are smooth, and it is pulled slowly enough that kinetic-energy changes can be ignored. Potential energy below the tabletop is negative only because of our chosen zero level.

Core1B · Different challenge

Pull a rope only partway up

A uniform 8 m rope has mass 4 kg. Initially 4 m hangs below a smooth platform edge. You pull very slowly until 2 m still hangs. There is no friction, stretching, or change in kinetic energy. Take g = 10 m/s². How much positive work do you do?

Skill 5 of 6 · Read graphs by their axes

📈 Read the axes first

Three graphs can look alike but mean very different things. Decide whether to find an area or a slope—and check the units.

Core1A · Guided example

Three graphs, three meanings

Imagine three notebook graphs. First read the vertical and horizontal axes. Then decide what operation answers the question.

  1. Graph A: force versus position. Force is +3 N from x = 0 to 2 m. The area is a rectangle: 3 × 2 = +6 J of work.
  2. Graph B: potential energy versus position. U falls from 9 J at x = 0 to 3 J at x = 2 m. The slope is (3 − 9) ÷ 2 = −3 J/m. The conservative force is the negative slope: +3 N.
  3. Graph C: displacement versus time. Displacement changes from 0 to 6 m over 3 s. The slope gives velocity: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m/s.
  4. Check the units: N × m = J for force–position area. J/m = N for potential–position slope. m/s is velocity for displacement–time slope.
  5. Say the choice first: “These axes show ___ against ___, so I use ___ because I need an answer in ___.”

Remember: a sloping line does not always mean a force, and an area does not always mean work. Read the axes before using a formula.

Why do signs and graph corners matter?

Work is signed when force acts with or against displacement. A conservative force equals minus the slope of its potential-energy graph. Velocity from a piecewise displacement–time plot is the slope within the chosen straight interval; at a sharp corner, a single instantaneous slope is not defined.

Skill 6 of 6 · Vertical circular motion

🪀 Stay on the circle?

A moving ball may have enough energy to reach a height but still lack the inward pull needed to follow a taut circular path.

Core1A · Guided example

A ball circling on a string

A 0.5 kg ball moves in a vertical circle on a light, taut 2 m string. At the top its speed is 6 m/s. Use g = 10 m/s²; ignore air resistance and pivot friction.

  1. At the top, inward is down. Gravity and string tension both act down. The needed inward force is mass × speed² ÷ radius.
  2. Find top tension: weight = 0.5 × 10 = 5 N. Needed inward force = 0.5 × 6² ÷ 2 = 9 N. So tension = 9 − 5 = +4 N. A positive tension is physically possible.
  3. Use energy from top to bottom: the top is twice the string length higher, or 4 m. Falling through 4 m gives the ball 0.5 × 10 × 4 = 20 J extra kinetic energy.
  4. Find bottom speed: v² at the bottom = 6² + 4 × 10 × 2 = 116. Thus speed ≈ 10.77 m/s.
  5. At the bottom, inward is up: tension acts up and gravity down. Tension − 5 = 0.5 × 116 ÷ 2 = 29 N, so bottom tension = 34 N.

Remember: energy tells you a possible speed, while the inward-force equation checks whether the string can actually keep the ball on the circle. At the top, tension cannot be negative.

What must I know before this advanced lesson?

This extension uses inward (radial) acceleration = speed² ÷ radius. Radial acceleration changes the direction of the velocity. Velocity itself is tangent to the circle; at the bottom it is horizontal, not vertically upward. The force equations apply only while the light string is taut. Gravity also changes speed during travel; there is no air resistance.

Core1B · Different challenge

Will a different string remain taut?

A 1 kg ball is attached to a light 1 m string with a fixed pivot. At the bottom it is moving at 7 m/s, tangent to the circle toward the rising side. The string begins taut. Use g = 10 m/s² and ignore air resistance.

Can the ball complete a full taut circular path? Check both the energy needed to reach the top height and the inward force the string must supply at the top. Explain what it means if the calculation gives a negative tension.
