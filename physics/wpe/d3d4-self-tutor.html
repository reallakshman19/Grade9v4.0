Skill 1 of 8 · Multi-force work and energy

🛴 Every force counts

A push helps, friction slows, gravity pulls—and your energy calculation must include them all.

Core1A · Guided example

A box being pulled across a floor

A 2 kg box already moves right at 1 m/s. A child pulls right with 10 N. Friction pulls left with 4 N. The box moves 3 m right on a level floor.

  1. Choose the object: the box. Draw the four forces and the direction of its movement.
  2. Do the signs first: pull helps movement (+), friction opposes it (−), gravity and normal do zero work during horizontal motion.
  3. Make a work ledger:
    ForceWork
    Pull+10 × 3 = +30 J
    Friction−4 × 3 = −12 J
    Gravity0 J
    Normal support0 J
  4. Add all the work: net work = +30 − 12 = +18 J. This—not friction alone—equals the change in kinetic energy.
  5. Find speed: initial K = ½ × 2 × 1² = 1 J, final K = 19 J. So final speed = √19 ≈ 4.36 m/s.

Remember: Every force gets a ledger row. Add the signed work before using the change in kinetic energy.

Model detective · when does a force do zero work?

Work depends on the angle between the force and displacement, not simply whether the force points up or down. A normal reaction on a fixed smooth ramp is perpendicular to the motion and does zero work; gravity does negative work during an uphill climb. For a constant force use W = Fd cos θ; when the force varies, account for its change along the path.

Common wrong move: subtract only friction and call that the change in kinetic energy. It is the sum of work by every force that equals ΔK.

When can I use this method?

The forces are constant over 3 m, air drag is ignored, and the box stays level. If it climbs upward, gravity also does negative work. The normal force is perpendicular to this horizontal movement, so its work is zero.

Core1B · Different challenge

Can the battery scooter climb?

A 3 kg toy scooter starts from rest at the bottom of a 2 m ramp. The top is 0.5 m higher. Its motor pushes 15 N uphill, while friction is 3 N downhill. The normal force is perpendicular to the ramp. Take g = 10 m/s². Does it reach the top, and how fast is it moving there?

Skill 2 of 8 · Spring contact and energy

🌀 When the spring stops pushing

One journey can have two different sets of forces. Find the point where the rules change.

Core1A · Guided example

A spring pushes a toy cart

A 1 kg cart begins at rest against a spring compressed by 0.20 m with stiffness 100 N/m. The cart is not attached. The whole track is smooth.

  1. Divide the path: A = spring contact. B = after the spring returns to natural length. The spring cannot pull this unattached cart.
  2. Energy at the start: spring energy = ½kx² = ½ × 100 × 0.20² = 2 J.
  3. At release: the 2 J becomes kinetic energy, so ½ × 1 × v² = 2. The cart leaves at 2 m/s.
  4. Later on smooth ground: the spring no longer acts and friction is zero, so the cart continues at 2 m/s.

Remember: Write one energy rule while contact exists, then check which forces act afterward. Do not continue adding spring work after separation.

Model detective · why not use one force everywhere?

While the spring is compressed, its force changes with compression; the released energy is ½kx², not one fixed force acting over the entire journey. For an unattached spring, contact ends at natural length. After that, a rough strip can remove kinetic energy through friction. If the spring is attached, it may pull as well as push and the boundary must be reconsidered.

Common wrong move: continue the spring force on the separate rough strip. Mark the change in contact first.

When can I use this method?

The spring is ideal and not attached, the track is smooth, and energy loss is ignored. If rough ground begins later, friction belongs in that later region only.

Core1B · Different challenge

The cart crosses a rough strip

A 2 kg toy cart begins at rest against an unattached spring of stiffness 200 N/m, compressed by 0.10 m. The launch is smooth until the spring reaches natural length. Then the cart immediately crosses a 0.50 m rough strip with 1 N friction. No motor acts. Does it cross, and what is its speed at the far end?

Skill 3 of 8 · Constant power

⚡ Energy added each second

A motor can supply equal amounts of energy every second without making speed rise equally every second.

Core1A · Guided example

A powered toy sled

A 2 kg sled already moves right at 2 m/s. A motor supplies a steady 12 watts of mechanical power on a smooth, level track, for 4 seconds. Assume there is no friction.

  1. Read watts: 12 W means the motor adds 12 J of energy per second, not 12 N of force.
  2. Start with energy already there: K at the start = ½ × 2 × 2² = 4 J.
  3. Keep an energy table: K after t seconds = 4 + 12 × t joules. At 1 s, K = 16 J. At 4 s, K = 52 J.
  4. Turn energy into speed: K = ½mv², so speed after 1 s is 4 m/s and after 4 s is √52 ≈ 7.21 m/s.
  5. Choose graph shapes: kinetic energy against time is a rising straight line. Speed against time rises but gradually flattens. Distance against time grows with an increasing slope because the sled speeds up.

Remember: constant net power into kinetic energy means equal kinetic-energy gain each second—not a constant force or constant acceleration. Always include the energy from the initial speed.

Model detective · different graphs, different slopes

For fixed mass, positive constant net mechanical power into kinetic energy P, and no other work contribution, dK/dt = P gives K(t) = K₀ + Pt. Since K = ½mv², speed follows v(t) = √(v₀² + 2Pt/m). The quoted power must be the actual rate of kinetic-energy gain—not a motor rating when friction or drag removes energy.

Read the axes: the slope of K–t is the constant P; the slope of v–t is acceleration, which falls as v rises; the slope of displacement–t is speed, which rises. These graphs cannot all be straight lines. At nonzero speed, P = Fv, so F = P/v and acceleration = P/(mv).

Different clue, different model: when a problem gives speed as a function of distance, first decide what its speed–distance relation implies about kinetic-energy change. Do not assume constant power simply because speed is changing. This contrast is useful when you return to the original question.

When does this work?

The supplied 12 W is useful mechanical power, the mass stays fixed, the sled is already moving, and there are no energy losses. We only need an energy table and graph shapes; no calculus or memorized distance–time power law is needed.

Skill 4 of 8 · Changing centre of mass

⛓️ Only the hanging part

When a rope hangs over an edge, the amount of hanging rope—and the position of its middle—both change as you pull.

Core1A · Guided example

Lift a chain onto a table

A uniform 4 m chain has mass 2 kg. Initially 2 m hangs below a smooth table edge; the rest lies on the table. Pull it very slowly until none hangs. Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. Find mass per metre: 2 kg ÷ 4 m = 0.5 kg/m.
  2. Use the hanging part: 2 m hangs, so its mass is 0.5 × 2 = 1 kg.
  3. Find its middle: because it is uniform, its centre of mass is halfway down—1 m below the table.
  4. Compare energies: choose table height as zero. Initially the hanging part has U = −1 × 10 × 1 = −10 J. At the end, U = 0 J.
  5. Find the work: the helper works slowly, so the work needed is the increase in potential energy: 0 − (−10) = +10 J.

Remember: as the hanging length shrinks, both its mass and its centre-of-mass depth shrink. Compare two pictures rather than lifting the entire chain as one fixed lump.

Model detective · why the work is not simply weight × height

For a uniform rope with mass per length λ, a hanging length ℓ has mass λℓ and a middle ℓ/2 below the tabletop. Relative to that tabletop its gravitational potential energy is U(ℓ) = −λgℓ²/2. A slow lift from ℓ₁ to ℓ₂ therefore needs λg(ℓ₁² − ℓ₂²)/2 work; both the hanging mass and its centre depth change.

If the rope accelerates while you pull, the work must also account for kinetic-energy change. For a nonuniform rope, constant λ is not valid.

When can I use this method?

The chain is uniform and flexible, the edge and tabletop are smooth, and it is pulled slowly enough that kinetic-energy changes can be ignored. Potential energy below the tabletop is negative only because of our chosen zero level.

Core1B · Different challenge

Pull a rope only partway up

A uniform 8 m rope has mass 4 kg. Initially 4 m hangs below a smooth platform edge. You pull very slowly until 2 m still hangs. There is no friction, stretching, or change in kinetic energy. Take g = 10 m/s². How much positive work do you do?

Skill 5 of 8 · Read graphs by their axes

📈 Read the axes first

Three graphs can look alike but mean very different things. Decide whether to find an area or a slope—and check the units.

Core1A · Guided example

Three graphs, three meanings

Imagine three notebook graphs. First read the vertical and horizontal axes. Then decide what operation answers the question.

  1. Graph A: force versus position. Force is +3 N from x = 0 to 2 m. The area is a rectangle: 3 × 2 = +6 J of work.
  2. Graph B: potential energy versus position. U falls from 9 J at x = 0 to 3 J at x = 2 m. The slope is (3 − 9) ÷ 2 = −3 J/m. The conservative force is the negative slope: +3 N.
  3. Graph C: displacement versus time. Displacement changes from 0 to 6 m over 3 s. The slope gives velocity: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 m/s.
  4. Check the units: N × m = J for force–position area. J/m = N for potential–position slope. m/s is velocity for displacement–time slope.
  5. Say the choice first: “These axes show ___ against ___, so I use ___ because I need an answer in ___.”

Remember: a sloping line does not always mean a force, and an area does not always mean work. Read the axes before using a formula.

Why do signs and graph corners matter?

Work is signed when force acts with or against displacement. A conservative force equals minus the slope of its potential-energy graph. Velocity from a piecewise displacement–time plot is the slope within the chosen straight interval; at a sharp corner, a single instantaneous slope is not defined.

Skill 6 of 8 · Vertical circular motion

🪀 Stay on the circle?

A moving ball may have enough energy to reach a height but still lack the inward pull needed to follow a taut circular path.

Core1A · Guided example

A ball circling on a string

A 0.5 kg ball moves in a vertical circle on a light, taut 2 m string. At the top its speed is 6 m/s. Use g = 10 m/s²; ignore air resistance and pivot friction.

  1. At the top, inward is down. Gravity and string tension both act down. The needed inward force is mass × speed² ÷ radius.
  2. Find top tension: weight = 0.5 × 10 = 5 N. Needed inward force = 0.5 × 6² ÷ 2 = 9 N. So tension = 9 − 5 = +4 N. A positive tension is physically possible.
  3. Use energy from top to bottom: the top is twice the string length higher, or 4 m. Falling through 4 m gives the ball 0.5 × 10 × 4 = 20 J extra kinetic energy.
  4. Find bottom speed: v² at the bottom = 6² + 4 × 10 × 2 = 116. Thus speed ≈ 10.77 m/s.
  5. At the bottom, inward is up: tension acts up and gravity down. Tension − 5 = 0.5 × 116 ÷ 2 = 29 N, so bottom tension = 34 N.

Remember: energy tells you a possible speed, while the inward-force equation checks whether the string can actually keep the ball on the circle. At the top, tension cannot be negative.

What must I know before this advanced lesson?

This extension uses inward (radial) acceleration = speed² ÷ radius. Radial acceleration changes the direction of the velocity. Velocity itself is tangent to the circle; at the bottom it is horizontal, not vertically upward. The force equations apply only while the light string is taut. Gravity also changes speed during travel; there is no air resistance.

Core1B · Different challenge

Will a different string remain taut?

A 1 kg ball is attached to a light 1 m string with a fixed pivot. At the bottom it is moving at 7 m/s, tangent to the circle toward the rising side. The string begins taut. Use g = 10 m/s² and ignore air resistance.

Can the ball complete a full taut circular path? Check both the energy needed to reach the top height and the inward force the string must supply at the top. Explain what it means if the calculation gives a negative tension.

Skill 7 of 8 · Flowing mass, momentum and motor power

📦 Momentum arrives every second

A conveyor must keep speeding up fresh material as it arrives. Find what happens each second—not the total mass already on the belt.

Core1A · Guided example

Sand joining a moving belt

A conveyor carries sand to the right at a steady 2 m/s. Each second 1 kg of sand lands with zero horizontal speed and leaves moving with the belt. Ignore other motor loads and vertical motion for this example.

  1. Think in kilograms per second: 1 kg arrives each second. Its horizontal speed changes from 0 to 2 m/s.
  2. Find the required push: momentum gain per second gives force = (1 kg/s) × (2 − 0 m/s) = 2 N right. The arriving sand pushes the belt backward by 2 N.
  3. Find extra motor power: to keep the belt at 2 m/s, the motor supplies 2 N × 2 m/s = 4 W for pickup.
  4. Separate the energy uses: new sand gains horizontal kinetic energy at ½ × 1 × (2² − 0²) = 2 W. The remaining 4 − 2 = 2 W becomes heat during slipping.
  5. Check what changes: if sand already arrived moving right at 2 m/s, the belt would need zero extra pickup force and power in this ideal model.

Remember: extra motor power and the sand's kinetic-energy gain are not always equal. Show the incoming velocity, the outgoing velocity, and how much material arrives per second.

When does this conveyor model work?

This is a steady-flow model of horizontal momentum transfer. Sand is slower than the belt and friction brings it to belt speed. The motor holds belt speed steady. We ignore vertical-energy changes, bearing losses, electrical inefficiency and other loads. The rates are extra pickup-related power, not total electricity consumed. A feeder's mass-per-second rate may depend on belt speed.

Core1B · Different challenge

Wood chips joining the school conveyor

A conveyor belt moves right at 4 m/s. Wood chips arrive already moving right at 1 m/s and leave at the belt speed. The feeder delivers half the numerical belt speed in kilograms per second: at 4 m/s, its rate is 4 ÷ 2 kg/s. Friction speeds up incoming chips. Assume steady flow and ignore all other loads and vertical changes.

Find the mass flow rate, required horizontal force, extra motor power, the rate of increase in the chips' horizontal kinetic energy, and the heat rate during pickup. Explain why the last three numbers must balance.

Skill 8 of 8 · Springs with different controlled variables

🌀 What stays the same?

A stiffer spring can store more energy in one experiment, but less in another. The answer depends on whether extension or final force is kept fixed.

Core1A · Guided example

Two springs, two kinds of fairness

Spring A is softer (100 N/m). Spring B is stiffer (200 N/m). Start from their natural lengths and pull slowly until they stop moving. The force builds gradually from zero.

  1. Start with Hooke's law: final force F = k × extension x. Elastic energy is ½kx², the triangular area under the increasing force–extension graph—not final F × x.
  2. Experiment A: hold extension fixed at 0.10 m. Spring A stores ½ × 100 × 0.10² = 0.50 J; spring B stores ½ × 200 × 0.10² = 1.00 J. The stiffer spring B stores more.
  3. Experiment B: hold final force fixed at 10 N. A extends 10 ÷ 100 = 0.10 m; B extends 10 ÷ 200 = 0.05 m. Their stored energies are 0.50 J in A and 0.25 J in B. The softer spring A stores more.
  4. Explain the reversal: at equal extension energy grows with stiffness. At equal final force, the stiffer spring moves less, and energy = F² ÷ (2k), so it stores less.

Remember: first label the test equal extension or equal final force. “Same force” here means the same final equilibrium force after slowly increasing from zero, not suddenly applying a constant force to a moving spring.

Why don't we multiply final force by extension?

The spring's resisting force increases gradually during loading from zero to its final value. The stored elastic energy is the triangular area: ½ × final force × extension. Both springs are ideal and Hookean, with no hysteresis, losses, overshoot or oscillation.

Skill 9 of 13 · E01 · Potential wells · Advanced extension

🏞️ Can it cross the energy hill?

A low-energy destination can be blocked by a high-energy barrier between here and there.

Core1A · Guided example

One smooth valley

A frictionless bead of 2 kg has potential energy U(x) = (x − 2)² J when x is measured in metres. Its total mechanical energy is 4 J. Only motion along the x-axis is allowed.

  1. Check the energy budget: kinetic energy K = E − U must be zero or positive. For x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 m, the potential values are 4, 1, 0, 1, 4 J.
  2. Find turning points: K = 0 at x = 0 and x = 4 m, the ends of one connected allowed interval.
  3. Find maximum speed: U is smallest at x = 2 m. Then K = 4 J, so with mass 2 kg, v = 2 m/s.
  4. Change the energy: at E = 1 J the accessible interval shrinks to 1–3 m. A bead cannot travel through a region where U exceeds its total E.

Remember: shade positions with E − U ≥ 0, then ask whether the shaded positions make a continuous path from the START.

What physics assumptions are needed?

One-dimensional conservative motion, no air resistance or friction, fixed total energy. An unstable energy-barrier summit at exact equality is a special limiting case; do not assume a finite-time crossing there.

Core1B · Different challenge

Two valleys with a central barrier

A different 2 kg bead moves without friction in a double-well potential U=(q² − 4)² J, where q = x/(1 m) is the numerical position measured in metres. It has total energy 9 J and starts at x = +2 m moving left. Can it reach the valley at x = −2 m? Find the allowed regions, inner and outer turning points for the right-hand valley, and maximum speed.

As starting clues only: U at −2 m and +2 m equals 0 J, but U at 0 m equals 16 J. Decide whether the whole connecting path is allowed—not just the destination.

Skill 10 of 13 · E02 · Instantaneous power · Vector extension

⚡ Power depends on direction

A moving object can have zero net power even while a nonzero force accelerates it.

Core1A · Guided example

Two forces, one direction

A trolley already moves right at 3 m/s. At this instant a person pulls with 6 N rightward and resistance acts with 2 N leftward.

  1. Power is a rate: at one instant the force's power is P = force · velocity. A force along motion adds kinetic energy; against motion, it removes energy.
  2. Give every force a sign: pull gives +6 × 3 = +18 W; resistance gives −2 × 3 = −6 W.
  3. Combine rates: net force is +4 N and net power = +4 × 3 = +12 W. That is the instantaneous rate of kinetic-energy change, not a promise of +12 J every later second.
  4. Check perpendicular forces: vertical gravity and normal support have zero power during horizontal travel.

Remember: force and velocity need matching direction components. Force magnitude × speed is valid only when aligned.

What should I know before this vector challenge?

This advanced task uses right/forward velocity components, signed component products and instantaneous mechanical power in watts (joules per second). If you have not learned vectors, start with the one-direction trolley and draw a table of right/forward arrows.

Core1B · Different challenge

A robot moving diagonally

At one instant a robot's velocity is 3 m/s right and 4 m/s forward. A motor pushes 6 N right. Resistance acts 2 N left and 1 N backward. Find motor power, resistance power, net power and the present kinetic-energy trend. Use signed rightward and forward components, not merely magnitudes.

Skill 11 of 13 · E03 · Spring launch and projectile phases

🌀 A spring stops pushing before landing

The energy calculation and the flight calculation belong to different phases.

Core1A · Two short guided examples

Spring speed, then independent flight time

Spring model A: a 1 kg puck begins at rest against an unattached spring (k = 100 N/m, compression 0.20 m) on a smooth track. Flight model B: a separate ball is already travelling horizontally at 3 m/s off a 1.25 m platform. Take g = 10 m/s².

  1. A · Contact energy: ½ × 100 × 0.20² = 2 J. At spring natural length contact ends; ½×1×v²=2 gives the puck 2 m/s.
  2. A · After separation: the unattached spring can no longer pull or push. On the smooth track this puck continues at 2 m/s.
  3. B · Vertical flight: the separate ball leaves horizontally with zero initial vertical speed; 1.25 = ½×10×t², so fall time 0.50 s.
  4. B · Horizontal flight: with no air resistance its x speed stays 3 m/s; landing range = 3×0.50 = 1.50 m.

Remember: identify when the spring ends, then join the correct energy and projectile models only when a new question needs BOTH.

Which conditions must hold?

An ideal Hookean spring, object not attached, no extra forces after spring release, horizontal launch from a known height, zero initial vertical velocity and negligible air resistance. The fresh task adds friction ONLY while spring contact exists.

Core1B · Different challenge

A dart leaves a rough spring launcher

A 0.50 kg dart begins at rest against, but not attached to, a horizontal spring with k=125 N/m compressed by 0.20 m. During the 0.20 m contact release, friction opposes motion with 2.5 N. At natural length it immediately leaves the 1.25 m high platform horizontally. Ignore air resistance; g=10 m/s². Find the energy at launch, launch speed, fall time and horizontal range.

Skill 12 of 13 · E04 · Repeated energy-losing bounces

🏀 Every bounce counts twice

A changing collision rule changes the first rebound and the geometric pattern that follows.

Core1A · Guided example

One retention rule for every impact

A ball drops vertically from rest at 2 m. At every ideal impact it loses 75% of kinetic energy and retains 25%. Ignore air drag. An infinite sequence of smaller rebounds is a mathematical enrichment, not a model of how real balls finally stop.

  1. After the first impact: 25% energy retained means first rebound height = 0.25×2 = 0.50 m.
  2. After later impacts: the same rule repeats, giving heights 0.125 m, 0.03125 m, …
  3. Count up AND down: total distance D = first 2 m drop + 2×(0.50+0.125+0.03125+…). Each complete rebound is counted twice.
  4. Sum the ideal series: height sum = 0.50/(1−0.25)=2/3 m. Total D = 2+2×(2/3) = 10/3 m ≈ 3.333 m.
  5. Check speed: speed after impact is √0.25 = 0.5 times impact speed, not 0.25. Energy depends on speed squared.

Remember: height follows the ENERGY retention fraction, speed follows its square root, and ideal rebound travel counts both directions.

Is an infinite number of bounces realistic?

No. The convergent geometric sum represents idealized vertical bounces without air drag and with a constant impact rule. Real balls eventually stop; this calculation is optional if geometric series have not yet been taught.

Core1B · Different challenge

First impact is different from the rest

A different ball starts at rest 2 m above the same type of level floor. At the first impact it retains 75% of its kinetic energy. At every later impact it retains just 50%. Find the first three rebound heights and the ideal total distance travelled, counting both upward and downward motion. Which percentage determines the first term, and which one determines the repeating ratio?

Skill 13 of 13 · E05 · Inward force, potential, circular motion

🪐 Area for potential, height for force

A force–radius graph answers two different physics questions depending on which operation you do.

Core1A · Guided example

An inward 3 N force

A 1 kg bead moves around an ideal horizontal circle of radius 2 m. A conservative central force points inward with magnitude 3 N at every positive radius. Let U(0)=0. Positive radial direction is outward; ignore drag and propulsion.

  1. Set arrows: force is inward, velocity tangent to the circle, so no work is done along the circular path.
  2. Potential from a radial comparison: signed radial force Fᵣ=−3 N, so dU/dr=+3 N. U(2)−U(0)=3×2 = +6 J.
  3. Circle from local force: 3 N = mv²/r = 1×v²/2. So v=√6 ≈ 2.45 m/s.
  4. Add energies: K=½×1×6=3 J and total mechanical energy E=U+K=9 J.
  5. Keep two operations separate: potential compares radial positions by an area; circular speed uses inward force at the chosen radius.

Remember: the positive magnitude of inward force plotted versus radius gives potential rise with increasing radius. On a signed radial-force graph, area gives the opposite sign. Neither graph area is the work done around a circle.

What must be true for these rules?

The central force is conservative, r is a positive outward radial coordinate and the chosen U(0)=0 is a limiting reference. At exactly r=0 “inward” has no fixed direction. A circular orbit remains at one fixed positive r with a tangential velocity and no radial work.

Core1B · Different challenge

A piecewise force–radius graph

A 2 kg spacecraft is in a steady circular orbit at r=3 m. The positive magnitude f(r) of a conservative INWARD force is 4 N from r=0 to 2 m, then grows linearly from 4 N at r=2 m to 8 N at r=4 m. Reference U(0)=0; positive r outward. Find U(3), force at 3 m, orbital speed, kinetic energy and total mechanical energy. Explain why you need the graph's area for U and its height for v.
