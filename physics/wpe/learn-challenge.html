🧲 Work and its sign

Decide whether a force adds energy, removes energy, or does no work.

01 · LEARN TO REASON Core1A candidate

A guide: the moving crate

A 10 N friction force acts left while a crate slides 3 m right. What work does friction do?

Work depends on the component of force along the displacement. For a constant force, W = Fd cos θ. Because friction and the motion point in opposite directions, θ = 180°.

  1. Choose the motion direction: 3 m right.
  2. Choose the force direction: 10 N left.
  3. Calculate W = 10 × 3 × cos 180° = −30 J.
  4. Interpret the sign: friction removes mechanical energy from the moving crate.
Three quick examples — same rule, different work

📦 Positive: Push a box to the right while it moves right. Your push does positive work.

🎒 Zero: Hold a bag up while walking horizontally. The upward supporting force is perpendicular to the horizontal movement, so that force does zero work.

🛞 Negative: Friction acting left as a wheel rolls right does negative work.

Net work means adding the work done by all forces; the work of friction alone is not necessarily the net work.

Contrast the three signs. Work is positive when a force has a component along displacement, negative when it opposes displacement, and zero when the force is perpendicular. For several forces or segments, calculate each signed contribution before adding; the work–energy theorem concerns net work, not one named force.
When does this reasoning apply?

Constant 10 N force, straight 3 m displacement, and no change of force direction within this step.

Watch out: Work does not become positive just because the object is moving.

🚀 Momentum and kinetic energy

Explain why equal momentum does not imply equal kinetic energy.

01 · LEARN TO REASON Core1A candidate

A guide: a toy trolley’s energy

A 2 kg toy trolley has momentum 6 kg·m/s. How much kinetic energy does it have?

Momentum tells us about motion: p = mv. Kinetic energy is energy due to motion: K = ½mv². Together, these give K = p²/(2m).

  1. Find the speed: v = p ÷ m = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 m/s.
  2. Find the energy: K = ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 J.
  3. Check using the shortcut: K = p²/(2m) = 36 ÷ 4 = 9 J.
  4. Notice the square: for the same mass, doubling momentum makes kinetic energy four times as large.

If you plot these points, you get a curve, not a straight line. Be careful: this pattern uses the same mass throughout.

Remember what stays the same! With the same mass, K grows with p². With the same momentum, a heavier cart has less kinetic energy. With the same kinetic energy, a heavier cart has more momentum magnitude: p = √(2mK). If both mass and momentum change, calculate K for each cart rather than guessing.
When does this reasoning apply?

Classical, nonrelativistic momentum; the stated momentum and mass are measured in one inertial frame.

Watch out: K is not proportional to p; for fixed mass, K grows with p².

⛰️ Energy and motion

Find where a moving object can go, where it stops, and why height matters.

01 · LEARN TO REASON Core1A candidate

A guide: a toy cart climbing a ramp

A 2 kg toy cart has 80 J of total mechanical energy on a smooth ramp. When it reaches a height of 2 m, how fast is it going? Take g = 10 m/s².

Think of the cart's energy as a fixed budget. On a smooth track, total energy = kinetic energy + potential energy: E = K + U, where U = mgh. As the cart climbs, it uses more of its budget for height and has less for speed.

  1. Energy stored by height: U = mgh = 2 × 10 × 2 = 40 J.
  2. Energy left for motion: K = E − U = 80 − 40 = 40 J.
  3. Find speed: K = ½mv², so v² = 2K/m = 40 and v ≈ 6.3 m/s.
  4. Check a boundary: at 4 m, U = 80 J, so K = 0 and the cart can momentarily stop. At 5 m, U = 100 J would exceed its 80 J budget, so that height is impossible without extra energy.

Quick check: what happens as the same cart climbs?

HeightHeight energy UMotion energy K
0 m0 J80 J
2 m40 J40 J
4 m80 J0 J
5 m100 JImpossible (K < 0)

Remember: K can be zero. It cannot be negative. To move with nonzero speed, K must be greater than zero.

What about a ball thrown straight up? Ignoring air resistance, gravity changes its vertical velocity by 10 m/s every second: for a launch at 20 m/s, after 1 second v = 10 m/s and after 2 seconds v = 0. But the ball rises 15 m in the first second and only 5 m in the second. Velocity changes uniformly with time, not with height. New situation to watch for: a destination might be low enough, yet a higher point on the way may still be impossible to reach. Check the whole path, not just the finish.
When does this reasoning apply?

Mechanical energy remains constant only when there are no losses or external energy inputs. Here the ramp is smooth, air resistance is ignored, the reference height is zero, and g = 10 m/s² is treated as constant. The vertical-ball time example also assumes no air drag.

Watch out: Even if the endpoint has U < E, any higher point in between could block the trip.

📉 Potential energy and force

Read an energy graph to find which way a force points.

01 · LEARN TO REASON Core1A candidate

A guide: follow a sloping energy line

On a straight track, a particle’s potential energy is 12 J at x = 0 m and 6 J at x = 2 m. The graph is a straight line. What force acts along the track?

Think of the graph as an energy map. In this one-dimensional conservative system, the force points opposite to the slope. If the energy line goes down as x increases, the force points right.

  1. Read two points: potential energy changes from 12 J to 6 J while position changes from 0 m to 2 m.
  2. Find the slope: (6 − 12) ÷ (2 − 0) = −3 J/m. Because 1 J/m = 1 N, this slope is −3 N.
  3. Reverse the sign for force: Fₓ = −(slope) = −(−3 N) = +3 N.
  4. Translate the sign: positive force means toward the right, the direction in which potential energy decreases.
Three clues for any straight section of the graph
Energy graph as x increasesForce points
↗ Rising← Left (negative)
→ FlatZero force
↘ FallingRight (positive) →

Remember: the sign of the force is opposite to the slope. This tells the force direction, not necessarily which way the particle is moving.

Why the minus sign? A conservative force tends toward lower potential energy. The more steeply the energy changes per metre, the larger the force magnitude. The short maths form is Fₓ = −dU/dx: for a straight segment, simply calculate −(change in U ÷ change in x). At a sharp corner, the ordinary derivative does not exist exactly at that point; you can still calculate each open straight segment separately.
When does this reasoning apply?

One-dimensional conservative forces and a potential-energy graph in the chosen x coordinate. Each straight segment has a defined slope, and the force is −dU/dx there. This is not a rule for all forces, such as friction, and a sharp corner requires special care.

Watch out: A positive potential energy is not the same as positive force. The change in potential energy is what matters.

💥 Push-apart carts and energy

Momentum stays balanced when carts push apart, even when their kinetic energy changes.

01 · LEARN TO REASON Core1A candidate

A guide: spring-powered toy carts

Two toy carts (1 kg and 3 kg) start at rest together on a smooth track. A compressed spring between them is released. The 1 kg cart rolls right at 3 m/s. Which way does the 3 kg cart move, and how fast?

Imagine two toy carts with a spring squeezed between them. When you let go, the spring pushes them apart. Momentum is conserved as long as there is negligible outside impulse. Because the carts started at rest, their total momentum started at zero.

  1. Choose a direction: call right positive (+) and left negative (−).
  2. Find the light cart’s momentum: p = mv = 1 × (+3) = +3 kg·m/s.
  3. Balance the total: 0 = +3 + (3 × v), so v = −1 m/s. The heavier cart moves left at 1 m/s.
  4. Check kinetic energy: before release both carts were still, so K = 0. Afterward K = ½ × 1 × 3² + ½ × 3 × 1² = 4.5 + 1.5 = 6 J. That extra kinetic energy came from the compressed spring.
One event, two different energy ideas
QuantityBeforeAfter
Total momentum0+3 + (−3) = 0
Total kinetic energy0 J6 J

Why does that make sense? Both carts gain motion, but their momenta cancel. Kinetic energy increases because stored spring energy is changed into motion. Total energy (including spring energy) is conserved when losses are negligible.

What if something was moving before the push-apart event? Always start with total momentum before = total momentum after. It is not automatically zero: calculate the starting momentum using p = mv. Work out each part's speed first, then use K = ½mv² to compare kinetic energies before and after. If motion energy rises, ask where the energy came from.
When does this reasoning apply?

The spring pushes only inside the two-cart system. We neglect any external horizontal impulse and friction, and use ordinary nonrelativistic speeds. “Right” is positive for all momentum calculations.

Watch out: Total momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy can change as stored spring energy turns into motion. Moving in the same direction does not mean two objects have the same momentum.
