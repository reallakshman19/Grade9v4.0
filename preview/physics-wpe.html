1. Fundamental Principles: Conservative Systems & Energy

In classical Newtonian mechanics, a force F is defined as conservative if the work done by the force in moving a particle between two points depends solely on the initial and final positions, and is completely independent of the trajectory taken:

∮F⋅dr=0⇔F=-∇U

In a one-dimensional system, this relates the force directly to the spatial derivative of the potential energy function U(x):

F(x)=-dUdx

The negative gradient indicates that the force always acts in the direction tending to decrease the potential energy, directing the system toward stable equilibrium points where dUdx=0 and d2Udx2>0.

2. Interactive Laboratory: Potential Well & Turning Points

Observe how mechanical energy is partitioned between potential energy U(x) and kinetic energy K(x) for a particle of mass m=1.0kg moving in a harmonic potential well U(x)=12kx2 with k=100.0N/m and total energy E=12.50J.

Potential Energy U(x) 0.00 J
Kinetic Energy K(x) 12.50 J
Instantaneous Speed v(x) 5.00 m/s

Notice: As x→±0.50m, U(x)→12.50J, meaning K(x)→0J and v→0m/s. These boundary points are the turning points of the motion.

3. The Work-Energy Theorem Invariance

The Work-Energy Theorem asserts that the net work done by all forces (conservative, non-conservative, and external) acting on a point particle equals the net change in its kinetic energy:

Wnet=∫xixfFnetdx=ΔK=Kf-Ki

When non-conservative forces (such as friction or drag) are absent, Wnet=Wc=-ΔU. Rearranging yields the fundamental law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy:

ΔK+ΔU=0⇒E=K+U=constant
